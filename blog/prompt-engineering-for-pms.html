写需求，是产品经理的日常；但把需求「喂」给 AI，能不能喂出好结果，却是一门值得专门练的手艺。同样是「帮我写个 PRD」，有人一句话甩过去，AI 还给你一份千篇一律的官话；有人把背景、边界、验收标准讲得明明白白，AI 交付的文档几乎可以直接评审。差别不在 AI，在提示词。

这篇文章，我用产品经理自己的语言，把「怎么写好一条需求提示词」讲透：先给你一个能记住的理论框架（六个问题），再带你现场改写一条真实需求，最后给两套可直接复制的完整示例。没有玄学，全是能上手的东西。

〇、为什么产品经理要会写提示词

先说结论：提示词工程，本质是「把产品经理脑子里的判断，翻译成 AI 能执行的指令」。AI 再强，也读不了心。你脑子里已经想清楚「谁是用户、为什么做、边界在哪、什么叫做好」，但如果你不写出来，AI 就只能猜。猜的结果，就是平庸、跑偏、返工。

一句话：写提示词不是技术活，是「把话说清楚」的表达活——这恰恰是产品经理的本行。

一、理论框架：六个问题，写清一条需求提示词

我把它总结成六个问题。写任何一条需求提示词，就是把这六个问题回答一遍。答得越具体，AI 交付得越准。

六个问题 = 一条高质量需求提示词的骨架

逐个展开说：

  1. 你是谁？——给 AI 一个角色。不指定角色，AI 就「平铺直叙」；指定角色，AI 就有了立场和视角。「资深产品经理」「目标用户」「技术架构师」，同一个任务，不同角色的输出质量完全不同。
  2. 发生了什么？——交代背景。用户是谁、什么行业、现在用什么、痛点在哪。没有背景，AI 只能套通用模板；有了背景，它才知道该往哪个方向使劲。
  3. 要达成什么？——一句话目标。用一句话说清这次要交付什么。目标越聚焦，AI 越不容易跑题。
  4. 你手里有什么？——提供输入。需求清单、访谈纪要、竞品截图、现有代码，统统可以喂给它。你给的信息越足，它越不用瞎编。
  5. 有什么不能做？——设定边界。范围、禁忌、不做的事。约束不是限制，是护栏——防止 AI 发挥过头、越做越宽。
  6. 交付成什么样？——规定输出。结构、格式、字数、语言、验收标准。先定义「什么叫做好」，AI 才能对齐交付。
问题常见错误正确做法
① 你是谁不设角色，输出没立场明确「你是资深产品经理 / 目标用户 / 架构师」
② 发生了什么只说任务，不给背景交代用户、行业、现状、痛点
③ 要达成什么目标含糊，多个任务混在一起一句话，聚焦单一交付物
④ 你手里有什么让 AI 凭空想象素材把清单、纪要、截图喂进去
⑤ 有什么不能做不给边界，AI 越做越宽写明范围、禁忌、不做的事
⑥ 交付成什么样只要「详细一点」，无验收标准规定结构、格式、字数、自查清单

二、现场演示：把一条模糊需求改写成结构化提示词

先拿一条「最像真实世界」的模糊需求开刀：

帮我写一个客户管理模块的需求文档，要详细一点，专业一点。

✗ 模糊需求 · 典型失败样本

这话丢给 AI，它只能一路猜：客户管理？ToB 还是 ToC？新功能还是改版？给谁看？写到多细？全是问号。AI 猜出来的，大概率是「看起来没错、哪都能用、哪都不贴」的通用模板。

用六个问题逐条追问，把答案填回去，就变成了下面这条：

你是资深产品经理，负责一款企业客户管理 SaaS 产品。【角色】
背景：销售获客靠手工记录，客户信息散落在 Excel 和微信群，
跟进无提醒，线索流失率高。【背景】
目标：产出一份「客户管理」功能模块的 PRD。【目标】
输入：功能清单（销售线索、客户档案、跟进记录、统计分析）+
2 条用户访谈纪要（见下文）。【输入】
约束：只写本模块范围；不写技术实现；每条功能标 P0/P1/P2。【约束】
输出：按「背景-目标-用户-功能清单-异常场景-验收标准」结构，
约 800 字，中文。【输出】

三、实践示例 1：用六问题写「PRD 需求文档」

这是 PM 最高频的场景。照着六个问题填空即可：

你是资深产品经理，负责一款企业客户管理 SaaS 产品。

【背景】目前销售获客靠手工记录，客户信息散落在 Excel 和微信群，
跟进无提醒，线索流失率高。

【目标】产出一份「客户管理」功能模块的 PRD。

【输入】功能清单：销售线索、客户档案、跟进记录、统计分析。
用户访谈纪要：
- 销售 A：我最想要的是「今天该跟进的客户」列表，不然总忘。
- 销售 B：客户信息能合并去重就好，现在重复录得头疼。

【约束】
- 只写本模块范围，不扩展到完整 CRM 流程
- 不写技术实现细节，只写产品逻辑
- 每条功能标注优先级 P0 / P1 / P2

【输出】按「背景 - 目标 - 用户 - 功能清单（含优先级）
- 异常场景 - 验收标准」结构输出，约 800 字，中文。

【自查】输出后自检：功能是否覆盖全部输入清单？
异常场景是否完整？缺失请补齐。

六个问题全覆盖 · 可直接复制使用

注意最后那条 【自查】——它让 AI 在交卷前自己先过一遍验收标准。这一条能把「半成品」挡在交付之前，效果立竿见影。

↺ 高质量交付 = 初始提示词 × 迭代轮数

  1. 给 AI 一个好例子（few-shot）。你希望它输出成什么样，先给一个样例。「按下面这个格式来：（示例）」——一个真实样例比十句描述都管用。
  2. 让 AI 先反问，再动手。在提示词末尾加一句「如果有不清楚的地方，先问我 3 个问题再开始」。AI 会把最影响交付的盲区问出来，你补完信息，它一次到位。
  3. 一轮一轮迭代，别指望一次到位。先让它出 80 分，再针对具体缺陷逐轮打磨。一次要求「全满」，它每个维度都只及格——这也是上一篇讲的「注意力管理」。
  4. 让 AI 交卷前自查。在输出里规定【自查】清单，让 AI 自己先过一遍验收标准。很多遗漏（空状态、异常场景、移动端）在这一步就被它自己拦下了。

六、避坑指南：五个常见错误

FIG 04 · 五个高频坑 / Five common pitfalls

✗ 只给任务不给背景

AI 乱猜 → 补上用户与痛点

✗ 一句话要求太多

注意力分散 → 一次一个目标

✗ 只要「详细一点」

没有验收标准 → 规定输出格式与自查

✗ 当一次性工具

不迭代 → 一轮轮反馈打磨

✗ 不给角色视角

输出没立场 → 指定专家角色

  1. 只给任务，不给背景。「写个登录页需求」——什么产品、给谁用、现在卡在哪？背景越少，AI 越只能套模板。
  2. 一句话要求太多。「又要写文档又要出原型又要做竞品分析」——一次塞三个任务，三个都做不好。拆成三条提示词，一条一个目标。
  3. 只要「详细一点、专业一点」。这是最无效的形容词。改成「按 X 结构输出，约 X 字，含异常场景和验收标准」，好坏立刻可判。
  4. 把 AI 当一次性工具。第一次输出不满意就换任务重来，等于每次都从零开始。应该把反馈喂回去，一轮比一轮准。
  5. 不给角色视角。不设角色，输出是「无主视角」的流水账；设了角色，它才会从「产品经理」「用户」「架构师」的立场替你把问题想全。

七、总结：把提示词当成你的「第二语言」

说到底，需求提示词就是产品经理的「第二语言」——把脑子里已经想明白的产品判断，翻译成 AI 听得懂的指令。

一句话公式：「你是（角色）。背景是（用户/现状/痛点）。目标是（一句话）。输入是（素材）。约束是（边界/禁忌）。请按（结构/格式）输出，并在交付前按（验收清单）自查。」

六个问题，一条模板，两套示例，四个技巧——这就是我从实践中沉淀下来的全部家当。下次再写需求提示词，先停下来，把六个问题在脑子里过一遍：角色、背景、目标、输入、约束、输出。你会发现，AI 的交付质量，肉眼可见地上了一个台阶。

Writing requirements is a product manager's daily job. But whether those requirements, once fed to AI, come back excellent — that is a craft worth practicing deliberately. Ask two people to "write a PRD." One fires off a single vague sentence and gets back boilerplate. The other lays out context, boundaries, and acceptance criteria clearly, and gets a document almost ready for review. The difference isn't the AI. It's the prompt.

In this article, I'll talk in a product manager's own language about how to write a good requirement prompt: first a theoretical framework you can actually remember (six questions), then a live rewrite of a real (vague) requirement, then two complete copy-paste-ready examples. No mysticism — just things that work.

0. Why a PM Should Learn to Write Prompts

Bottom line: prompt engineering is about translating the judgments in a product manager's head into instructions AI can execute. No matter how strong the AI, it can't read your mind. You already know who the users are, why you're building this, where the boundaries lie, and what "good" looks like — but if you don't write it down, AI has to guess. Guessing yields mediocrity, drift, and rework.

In one sentence: writing prompts isn't a technical skill — it's an expression skill of "saying things clearly," which is exactly a product manager's home turf.

1. The Framework: Six Questions That Write a Requirement Prompt

I've compressed it into six questions. To write any requirement prompt, answer these six. The more specific your answers, the closer AI lands.

① Who are you?

Role · give AI a viewpoint and stance

→

② What happened?

Context · users, industry, status, pain points

→

③ What's the goal?

Goal · one sentence for this deliverable

④ What do you have?

Input · lists, materials, interviews

→

⑤ What mustn't you do?

Constraint · scope, boundaries, taboos

→

⑥ What should it look like?

Output · structure, format, acceptance

Six questions = the skeleton of a high-quality requirement prompt

Let's unpack each:

  1. Who are you? — give AI a role. Without a role, AI just narrates flatly. With a role, it gains a stance and a viewpoint. "Senior PM," "target user," "technical architect" — the same task yields very different quality depending on the role.
  2. What happened? — set the context. Who are the users, what industry, what do they use now, where does it hurt. Without context, AI reaches for generic templates; with context, it knows which direction to push.
  3. What's the goal? — one-sentence objective. State in one line what this task must deliver. The more focused the goal, the less AI drifts.
  4. What do you have? — provide input. Requirement lists, interview notes, competitor screenshots, existing code — feed it all in. The more you give, the less it has to invent.
  5. What mustn't you do? — set boundaries. Scope, taboos, things not to do. Constraints aren't limits; they're guardrails that stop AI from overreaching.
  6. What should it look like? — specify the output. Structure, format, length, language, acceptance criteria. Define "what good means" first, and AI can align its delivery.

2. Live Demo: Rewriting a Vague Requirement into a Structured Prompt

Start with a vague requirement that looks painfully familiar:

Write me a requirements document for a customer-management module.
Make it detailed and professional.

✗ Vague requirement · a classic failure sample

Thrown at AI, this is a guessing game: customer management? B2B or B2C? New feature or redesign? Who reads it? How detailed? All question marks. What AI guesses is usually a generic template that "looks right, works anywhere, fits nowhere."

Run each of the six questions, fill in the answers, and it becomes:

You are a senior PM for a B2B customer-management SaaS. [Role]
Context: sales acquires leads by hand; customer info is scattered
across Excel and WeChat; follow-ups have no reminders, so lead
attrition is high. [Context]
Goal: produce a PRD for the "Customer Management" module. [Goal]
Input: feature list (leads, customer profiles, follow-ups,
analytics) + 2 interview notes (below). [Input]
Constraints: only this module's scope; no technical implementation
detail; tag every feature P0/P1/P2. [Constraint]
Output: structure as "Context-Goal-Users-Features-Edge cases-
Acceptance," about 800 words, in English. [Output]

✓ Structured prompt · all six questions answered · each line maps to one

3. Example 1: Writing a PRD with the Six Questions

This is the PM's most frequent scenario. Just fill in the blanks:

You are a senior PM for a B2B customer-management SaaS product.

[Context] Sales acquires leads by hand; customer info is scattered
across Excel and WeChat; follow-ups have no reminders and lead
attrition is high.

[Goal] Produce a PRD for the "Customer Management" feature module.

[Input] Feature list: sales leads, customer profiles, follow-up
records, statistics & analytics.
User interview notes:
- Sales rep A: "What I want most is a 'follow up today' list —
  otherwise I keep forgetting."
- Sales rep B: "Deduping customer profiles would save me — I'm
  re-entering the same records, and it's a headache."

[Constraints]
- Only this module's scope; don't expand to the full CRM flow
- No technical implementation detail; product logic only
- Tag every feature P0 / P1 / P2

[Output] Structure as "Context - Goal - Users - Features (with
priority) - Edge cases - Acceptance criteria," about 800 words.

[Self-check] After writing, verify: does it cover every item in
the input list? Are edge cases complete? Fill in anything missing.

All six questions covered · copy-paste ready

Note that last line, [Self-check] — it makes AI run through the acceptance criteria before handing in. That single line stops half-finished work at the door, and the effect is immediate.

Check acceptance

Verify against the criteria you set

↺ High-quality delivery = initial prompt × iteration rounds

  1. Give AI a good example (few-shot). Want it to output a certain shape? Show it one sample first. "Follow this format: (sample)" — one real example beats ten sentences of description.
  2. Let AI ask before it acts. End the prompt with "If anything is unclear, ask me 3 questions before you start." AI surfaces the blind spots that matter most; you fill them in, and it nails it in one pass.
  3. Iterate round by round; don't expect perfection at once. Let it deliver an 80% version, then polish specific defects. Ask for "everything at once" and every dimension lands at passing — that's the attention management from the previous post.
  4. Have AI self-check before handing in. Put a [Self-check] checklist in the output spec so AI runs through the acceptance criteria itself. A lot of omissions (empty states, edge cases, mobile) get caught by AI before they reach you.

Flat output → assign an expert role

  1. Task only, no context. "Write requirements for a login page" — what product, who uses it, what's stuck today? Less context, and AI can only fall back on templates.
  2. Too many asks in one sentence. "Write the doc, make the prototype, and do the competitive analysis" — three tasks in one shot, three done poorly. Split into three prompts, one goal each.
  3. Just "detailed and professional." The most useless adjectives. Replace with "output as X structure, about X words, including edge cases and acceptance criteria," and quality becomes judgeable instantly.
  4. Treating AI as a one-shot tool. Give up on the first output and start a new task, and you restart from zero every time. Feed the feedback back in instead — each round lands closer.
  5. No role, no viewpoint. Without a role, output reads like an ownerless log. With a role, AI thinks through the problem from the stance of a PM, a user, or an architect.

7. Summary: Make the Prompt Your "Second Language"

At the end of the day, a requirement prompt is a product manager's "second language" — translating the product judgments already in your head into instructions AI understands.

One-line formula: "You are (role). Context: (users/status/pain). Goal: (one sentence). Input: (materials). Constraints: (boundaries/taboos). Output as (structure/format), and self-check against (acceptance list) before delivering."

Six questions, one template, two examples, four techniques — that's the whole toolkit I've distilled from practice. Next time you write a requirement prompt, pause and run the six questions through your head: role, context, goal, input, constraint, output. You'll find AI's delivery quality climbs a visible step.
